Part I · Modes of convergence

01

Four ways to converge

For a deterministic sequence "\(x_n\to x\)" has one meaning. A random sequence \(X_n(\omega)\) is a whole family of trajectories, one per outcome \(\omega\in\Omega\), so we can ask different questions about it: does every single trajectory settle? does the probability of being far go to zero? does the average squared error vanish? or does only the shape of the distribution approach a limit?

modedefinitionwhat it looks atwhere it is used
almost sure \(X_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}X\)\(P\big(\{\omega:\lim_n X_n(\omega)=X(\omega)\}\big)=1\) each trajectory, one at a time: all of them converge except a set of probability 0strong LLN; Glivenko–Cantelli (the empirical CDF converges uniformly)
in probability \(X_n\xrightarrow{P}X\)\(\forall\varepsilon>0:\ P(|X_n-X|>\varepsilon)\to0\) the mass at a fixed time n: less and less of it is far from Xweak LLN; consistency of estimators
mean square \(X_n\xrightarrow{L^2}X\)\(E\big[(X_n-X)^2\big]\to0\) the average squared distance, i.e. variance + bias² of an estimatorLLN by Chebyshev (\(\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_n)=\sigma^2/n\)); MSE of estimators
in distribution \(X_n\xrightarrow{d}X\)\(F_{X_n}(x)\to F_X(x)\) at every continuity point of \(F_X\) only the law, not the values: \(X_n\) and \(X\) need not even live on the same \(\Omega\)CLT; asymptotic tests and confidence intervals; Slutsky, delta method
Implications

Every arrow that is missing has a counterexample, and section 02 simulates one for each.

Roles in the two great theorems

LLN. For i.i.d. \(X_i\) with mean \(\mu\): the weak law says \(\bar X_n\xrightarrow{P}\mu\), the strong law (Kolmogorov) says \(\bar X_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}\mu\). With finite variance the proof seen in the lectures goes through \(L^2\): \(E(\bar X_n-\mu)^2=\sigma^2/n\to0\), and Chebyshev turns it into convergence in probability. The strong law is the statement a single long experiment relies on: your trajectory converges, not just most of them at each fixed time.

CLT. \(Z_n=\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)/\sigma\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal N(0,1)\). It is only in distribution: \(Z_n\) keeps fluctuating forever (it does not converge in probability to anything), but its histogram settles on the bell. This is enough for inference, because tests and intervals only use probabilities, i.e. the law.

Other results. Consistency of an estimator is convergence in probability to the parameter; Slutsky's theorem lets us replace \(\sigma\) by \(s\) (which converges in probability) inside a quantity converging in distribution; the posterior distribution itself concentrates on the true parameter as data grow (section 07).

03

Why √n, and when it breaks

The derivation in three lines

Two properties of the variance, both from its quadratic definition:

$$\mathrm{Var}(aX)=a^2\,\mathrm{Var}(X),\qquad \mathrm{Var}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\Big)=\sum_i\mathrm{Var}(X_i)+2\sum_{i<j}\mathrm{Cov}(X_i,X_j).$$

If the \(X_i\) are independent (or just uncorrelated) the covariances vanish, so \(\mathrm{Var}(S_n)=n\sigma^2\). To keep the spread of \(S_n/c_n\) constant we need \(n\sigma^2/c_n^2=\sigma^2\), i.e. \(c_n=\sqrt n\). Dividing by \(n\) is too much (variance \(\sigma^2/n\to0\): LLN), dividing by 1 is too little (\(n\sigma^2\to\infty\)), \(\sqrt n\) is the balance point where the CLT finds a non-degenerate limit.

In inference the same fact gives the standard error \(\sigma/\sqrt n\): to halve the width of a confidence interval you need four times the data.

The hidden assumption: no correlation

The √n law is a consequence of the covariances being zero. Real security data are rarely like that: traffic comes in bursts, a scanner sends many similar requests, one compromised host generates correlated alerts. For a stationary AR(1) process with lag-one correlation \(\rho\) (unit variance),

$$\mathrm{Var}(S_n)\approx n\,\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho},$$

still linear in \(n\), but with a larger constant: the textbook interval \(\bar x\pm1.96\,s/\sqrt n\) becomes too narrow and covers the truth far less than 95% of the time. At the extreme, if all \(X_i\) are the same variable, \(\mathrm{Var}(S_n)=n^2\sigma^2\): averaging gives no information at all.

Part II · Conditional probability and Bayesian inference

04

Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem

In statistics: conditional frequencies

In a contingency table (HW6) a conditional distribution is one column read on its own: \(f(x_i\mid y_j)=n_{ij}/n_{\cdot j}\). We restrict the population to the units with \(Y=y_j\) and recompute the proportions inside it. Reading the same cell by row gives the other conditional, \(n_{ij}/n_{i\cdot}\): the two are different numbers.

In probability: a definition

$$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)},\qquad P(B)>0 .$$

It restricts \(\Omega\) to \(B\) and renormalizes so that \(B\) has probability 1. Since \(A\cap B=B\cap A\),

$$P(A\mid B)P(B)=P(B\mid A)P(A)\ \Longrightarrow\ P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}.$$

Bayes as an inference engine

Algebraically trivial, conceptually central. Replace \(A\) by an unknown parameter or cause \(\theta\) and \(B\) by the observed data \(x\):

$$\underbrace{p(\theta\mid x)}_{\text{posterior}}=\frac{\overbrace{p(x\mid\theta)}^{\text{likelihood } L(\theta)}\ \overbrace{p(\theta)}^{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{p(x)}_{\text{evidence}}},\qquad p(x)=\sum_\theta p(x\mid\theta)\,p(\theta),$$

or, comparing two hypotheses, in odds form:

$$\underbrace{\frac{p(\theta_1\mid x)}{p(\theta_2\mid x)}}_{\text{posterior odds}}=\underbrace{\frac{p(x\mid\theta_1)}{p(x\mid\theta_2)}}_{\text{Bayes factor}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{p(\theta_1)}{p(\theta_2)}}_{\text{prior odds}}.$$
  • Prior \(p(\theta)\): what we believe before the data (base rates, last month's logs, expert knowledge, or deliberate ignorance).
  • Likelihood \(L(\theta)=p(x\mid\theta)\): how probable the observed data are if \(\theta\) were true. It is a function of \(\theta\) but not a probability distribution over \(\theta\) (it need not sum to 1).
  • Posterior \(p(\theta\mid x)\): the updated belief. Tomorrow's data can update it again, with today's posterior as the new prior: this is why Bayesian learning is naturally an online algorithm.

The lecture's warning: likelihood ≠ posterior. A cough is almost certain if you have a terminal illness and only 50% likely with a flu, so the illness has the higher likelihood; but it is so rare that the flu keeps by far the higher posterior. With a uniform prior the two coincide up to normalization (section 06).

05

Evidence meter: one alarm is not proof

A detector flags malicious traffic. In odds form, each alarm multiplies the odds of "attack" by the likelihood ratio \(LR=\frac{P(\text{alarm}\mid\text{attack})}{P(\text{alarm}\mid\text{benign})}\). On a log scale the multiplication becomes addition: Alan Turing and I. J. Good measured evidence in decibans, \(10\log_{10}(\text{odds})\), when breaking Enigma at Bletchley Park. Several independent sensors (in the sense of HW6, conditionally on the true state) simply add their decibans.

06

Bayes breaks Caesar

Back to the first homeworks: a message encrypted with an unknown shift \(\theta\in\{0,\dots,25\}\). If the plaintext letters follow English frequencies \(q_\ell\), the likelihood of a ciphertext \(c_1\dots c_k\) is \(L(\theta)=\prod_i q_{(c_i-\theta)\bmod 26}\), and the posterior over the 26 keys is updated one letter at a time. Reveal more letters and watch the posterior collapse on one key.

07

Attack rate: how much does the prior matter?

A server receives a Poisson number of intrusion attempts per hour with unknown rate \(\lambda\). After \(n\) hours with counts \(k_1,\dots,k_n\) the likelihood is \(L(\lambda)\propto\lambda^{\sum k_i}e^{-n\lambda}\). The posterior is computed numerically on a grid of 1,200 values of λ, so any prior works, even non-standard ones. Four analysts start from four different priors and see the same data.

08

Advantages, limitations and influence on conclusions

Advantages

  • Uses what we know. Base rates are knowledge: ignoring them is the base-rate fallacy of section 05, where a 99%-accurate detector is wrong most of the time it fires.
  • Direct answers. The posterior gives \(P(\theta\in[a,b]\mid x)\), the statement people usually want and wrongly read into a frequentist confidence interval.
  • Sequential by nature. Yesterday's posterior is today's prior: alerts, letters or hours can be absorbed one at a time without storing the past.
  • Stable with little data. A reasonable prior shrinks wild estimates from a handful of observations (in section 07, after one hour with only 2 attempts, the flat analyst estimates λ ≈ 3 while the informative one already says 6.3, against a true value of 7).

Limitations

  • Subjectivity. With little data the conclusion depends on the prior: two analysts can disagree honestly. Reporting the posterior under several priors (sensitivity analysis) is the remedy.
  • Wrong priors cost data. A confident wrong prior needs many observations to be overturned (the "confidently wrong" analyst), and a prior that gives probability 0 to the truth can never recover: Cromwell's rule, shown by the dogmatic prior in section 06.
  • Model dependence. The likelihood assumes a model (English letter frequencies, Poisson counts, independent sensors). If sensors are correlated, multiplying their likelihood ratios overstates the evidence.
  • Computation. \(p(x)\) is an integral over all parameters; with many parameters it needs MCMC or variational methods instead of a simple grid.

The two parts meet

As data accumulate, every posterior that gives positive weight to the truth concentrates on it, with a width that shrinks like \(1/\sqrt n\) (Bernstein–von Mises: asymptotically it is a normal centred at the MLE with the frequentist standard error). The posterior consistency of section 07 is a convergence in probability, and its \(\sqrt n\) is the same variance additivity of section 03. This is why Bayesian and frequentist answers agree with large samples and can differ sharply with small ones, which is exactly where the prior matters.
