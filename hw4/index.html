Ex.1 · Deriving Welford's recurrences

Notation: \(x_1,x_2,\dots\) is the stream of scores, \(M_k\) the mean of the first \(k\) values, \(SS_k=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(x_i-M_k)^2\) their sum of squared deviations, and \(\delta_k=x_k-M_{k-1}\) the "surprise" of the new value. Knuth's derivation of the mean uses three moves; we apply the same three moves to \(SS_k\). The only extra tool is a centre-shift rule, the same one that in mechanics is called the parallel-axis (Steiner) theorem.

The three moves, side by side

movemean \(M_k\) (Knuth)sum of squares \(SS_k\) (Welford)
① split off the last value \(kM_k=\sum_{i<k}x_i+x_k\) \(SS_k=\sum_{i<k}(x_i-M_k)^2+(x_k-M_k)^2\)
② write the old part with the old state \(\sum_{i<k}x_i=(k-1)M_{k-1}\) \(\sum_{i<k}(x_i-M_k)^2=SS_{k-1}+(k-1)(M_k-M_{k-1})^2\)  (centre shift, below)
③ isolate the increment \(M_k=M_{k-1}+\dfrac{\delta_k}{k}\) \(SS_k=SS_{k-1}+\delta_k\,(x_k-M_k)\)

Move ② · the centre-shift rule

The first \(k-1\) scores have their squares measured from \(M_{k-1}\), but \(SS_k\) needs them measured from the new centre \(M_k\). Write each deviation through the old centre, with \(d=M_k-M_{k-1}\):

$$\sum_{i<k}(x_i-M_k)^2=\sum_{i<k}\big[(x_i-M_{k-1})-d\big]^2 =SS_{k-1}-2d\underbrace{\sum_{i<k}(x_i-M_{k-1})}_{=\,0}+(k-1)\,d^2 .$$

The cross term vanishes because deviations from their own mean always sum to zero. So moving the reference point by \(d\) adds (number of points)\(\times d^2\), exactly like the moment of inertia of \(k-1\) unit masses when the axis is moved away from their centre of mass.

What happens when score xₖ arrives

The new score pulls the centre by only \(1/k\) of the surprise. The old points pay \((k-1)d^2\) for the move, the new point contributes \((x_k-M_k)^2\).

Move ③ · putting the pieces together

  1. From Knuth: \(d=M_k-M_{k-1}=\dfrac{\delta_k}{k}\) and \(x_k-M_k=\delta_k-\dfrac{\delta_k}{k}=\dfrac{k-1}{k}\,\delta_k\).
  2. Insert both into ① and ②: $$SS_k=SS_{k-1}+(k-1)\frac{\delta_k^2}{k^2}+\frac{(k-1)^2}{k^2}\,\delta_k^2 =SS_{k-1}+\frac{(k-1)\,\delta_k^2}{k^2}\,(1+k-1)=SS_{k-1}+\frac{k-1}{k}\,\delta_k^2 .$$
  3. Recognise \(\frac{k-1}{k}\delta_k=x_k-M_k\) and split \(\delta_k^2\) into its two factors:
    $$SS_k=SS_{k-1}+(x_k-M_{k-1})\,(x_k-M_k),\qquad SS_0=0,\ M_0=0$$

Two equivalent forms come out for free: \(SS_k=SS_{k-1}+\frac{k-1}{k}\delta_k^2\) shows that SS can never decrease, while the product form is the one used in code because it needs no extra multiplication by \(\frac{k-1}{k}\).

Recurrence for the variance

The sample variance is \(s_k^2=SS_k/(k-1)\) for \(k\ge2\). Dividing the boxed result by \(k-1\) and using \(SS_{k-1}=(k-2)s_{k-1}^2\):

$$s_k^2=\frac{k-2}{k-1}\,s_{k-1}^2+\frac{\delta_k^2}{k},\qquad k\ge 2\quad(\text{so } s_2^2=\delta_2^2/2).$$

With divisor \(k\) instead (\(v_k=SS_k/k\)): \(\;v_k=\frac{k-1}{k}\,v_{k-1}+\frac{k-1}{k^2}\,\delta_k^2 .\)

In code it is simpler to keep \(M_k\) and \(SS_k\) as state and divide only when a variance is requested.

Ex.1 · The code: three lines

The state is three numbers: how many scores have been absorbed, their centre and their sum of squares. After the counter is increased, the core logic is the three lines that mirror moves ①–③.

// s = { n: 0, avg: 0, ss: 0 }
function absorb(s, x) {
  s.n++;
  const gap = x - s.avg;     // surprise w.r.t. the old centre
  s.avg += gap / s.n;          // the centre moves by 1/n of it
  s.ss  += gap * (x - s.avg);  // old gap × new gap
}
const sampleVar = s => s.ss / (s.n - 1);   // divisor n−1
# s = {"n": 0, "avg": 0.0, "ss": 0.0}
def absorb(s, x):
    s["n"] += 1
    gap = x - s["avg"]            # surprise w.r.t. the old centre
    s["avg"] += gap / s["n"]       # the centre moves by 1/n of it
    s["ss"]  += gap * (x - s["avg"]) # old gap × new gap

sample_var = lambda s: s["ss"] / (s["n"] - 1)  # divisor n−1

The order of the lines is the derivation: gap is \(\delta_k\), computed with the centre before the update; the last line multiplies it by the distance from the centre after the update. Memory is constant and every score is read once, so the same function can sit on a live stream of IDS events or log lines that are never stored. It is also numerically safer than the one-pass shortcut \(\sum x^2-(\sum x)^2/n\), which subtracts two huge, almost equal numbers.

04

Ex.1 · Beyond one stream: merging sensors

In a real SOC the attempts of one attacker are seen by several sensors (firewall, IDS, WAF…), each keeping its own {n, avg, ss}. The centre-shift rule of move ② also tells how to merge two states without seeing any raw score. For groups A and B with \(\Delta=M_B-M_A\) and \(n=n_A+n_B\):

$$M_{AB}=M_A+\Delta\,\frac{n_B}{n},\qquad SS_{AB}=SS_A+SS_B+\Delta^2\,\frac{n_A\,n_B}{n}.$$

Each group pays its own centre shift (move ② applied to A and to B). Welford's update is the special case of a group B made of a single score: \(n_B=1,\ SS_B=0,\ \Delta=\delta_k\), which gives back \(SS_k=SS_{k-1}+\frac{k-1}{k}\delta_k^2\).

Ex.2 · The score distribution

Each penetration attempt gets an integer score \(X\in\{0,1,\dots,10\}\) (0 = repelled, 10 = full compromise) drawn from a fixed pmf \(p_0,\dots,p_{10}\). Its theoretical moments are

$$\mu=\sum_{j=0}^{10}j\,p_j,\qquad \sigma^2=\sum_{j=0}^{10}(j-\mu)^2p_j,\qquad \mu_4=\sum_{j=0}^{10}(j-\mu)^4p_j .$$

The default is a hardened-vs-exposed mixture: most systems are patched and yield low scores, a minority is exposed and yields high ones, so the law is bimodal and skewed, far from a bell curve. Pick another preset, or drag the bars to draw your own distribution.

Ex.2 · How n changes the variability

Unbiased does not mean precise. The spread of the estimators across hackers shrinks as \(n\) grows, following known laws:

$$\mathrm{Var}(\bar x_n)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n},\qquad \mathrm{Var}(s_n^2)=\frac{1}{n}\left(\mu_4-\frac{n-3}{n-1}\,\sigma^4\right)\;\approx\;\frac{\mu_4-\sigma^4}{n}.$$

In other words \(\bar x_n\) and \(s_n^2\) are unbiased: \(E[\bar x_n]=\mu\) and \(E[s_n^2]=\sigma^2\) for each \(n\), not just in the limit.

The divisor matters. Each hacker measures squares from their own centre \(\bar x\), which is the point that makes the sum of squares smallest, so \(SS_n\) runs short. The centre-shift rule of Ex.1 quantifies it: \(\sum(x_i-\mu)^2=SS_n+n(\bar x-\mu)^2\), and taking expectations \(n\sigma^2=E[SS_n]+\sigma^2\), so \(E[SS_n]=(n-1)\sigma^2\). Dividing by \(n\) instead would give at n = (theory ), the hollow points in the second chart.

Why is this useful in inferential statistics?

In a real assessment we observe one attacker, one audit, one week of logs, never the whole population of attempts. We still want μ (how successful attacks are on average) and σ² (how unpredictable they are). Unbiasedness means the recipe is aimed at the right target: repeated over many studies, errors cancel instead of piling up. The variance laws then say how far one study can miss: the standard error \(s_n/\sqrt n\) is computed from the same single sample, and with the CLT of HW3 it gives confidence intervals and tests.

But the averages over m hackers hide the experience of a single hacker. At n = , of the hackers underestimate σ², because the distribution of \(s^2\) is right-skewed: many small values balanced by a few large ones. A single small sample tends to make attacks look more predictable than they are.

Effect of the number of systems n

Raising n does not move the centres, which are already right: it tightens both clouds. From n = to n = the spread of the sampling means goes from to (theory σ/√n: → ), the spread of the sampling variances from to . Both follow \(1/\sqrt n\), so halving the error costs four times the systems.

How many systems are "enough" depends on the law, not only on n. The precision of \(\bar x\) depends on \(\sigma^2\); the relative precision of \(s^2\) depends on the kurtosis \(\mu_4/\sigma^4\). With the current law the audit-budget targets need systems for the mean and for the variance; for laws with rare extreme scores (e.g. "rare jackpot") the variance becomes by far the more expensive quantity, because a handful of full compromises dominates the sum of squares.

m, the number of hackers, has a different role: it does not improve any single estimate, it only sharpens our view of the sampling distributions (averages, histograms), with Monte-Carlo error ∝ 1/√m.

Implementation notes

  • One update everywhere. Every per-hacker statistic is produced by the same absorb of section 02; the averages and spreads across hackers are computed with it too, and the sensor demo uses the merge formula of section 04.
  • Sampling a discrete law. Scores are drawn by inverse transform on the cumulative pmf of the 11 values.
  • Independent repetitions. Each value of n is a fresh experiment with new trajectories, as the assignment asks.
  • Reproducibility. The seed string is hashed (FNV-1a) into a mulberry32 PRNG, with a separate sub-stream for each n, so the same seed and law always give the same tables.
  • Exact theory. μ, σ², μ₄, Var(s²) and the audit budget are computed exactly from the pmf.
  • No chart library. All plots are drawn by hand on Canvas 2D (HiDPI-aware); only KaTeX is loaded for formulas.
