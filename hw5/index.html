Two Ways to Slice an Area, and a Number with No Formula

Riemann cuts the domain into columns, Lebesgue cuts the range into layers and measures what lies underneath. We compute both, bracket the true value from below and above, find where they part ways, and then compute the normal CDF, an integral that no elementary formula can express.

Ex.1 · Columns vs layers

Riemann: partition the domain

Cut \([a,b]\) into \(N\) pieces \(I_i\) of width \(\Delta x\). On each piece take the lowest and the highest value of \(f\): the Darboux sums trap the area between two staircases.

$$L_N=\sum_i \inf_{I_i} f\;\Delta x\;\le\;\int_a^b f\;\le\;U_N=\sum_i \sup_{I_i} f\;\Delta x$$

\(f\) is Riemann-integrable exactly when the gap \(U_N-L_N\) can be made as small as we like. A cheap point estimate in between is the midpoint sum \(\sum_i f(m_i)\Delta x\).

Lebesgue: partition the range

Cut the values \([y_{\min},y_{\max}]\) into \(M\) levels \(y_j\) of height \(\Delta y\). For each level, ask how much of the domain lies above it: the measure of the preimage \(\{f>y\}\).

$$\int f\,d\mu = y_{\min}(b-a)+\int_{y_{\min}}^{y_{\max}}\mu(\{x:f(x)>y\})\,dy$$

Since \(y\mapsto\mu(\{f>y\})\) is decreasing, the layers also give a lower and an upper bound, and their gap is at most \(\Delta y\,(b-a)\) for every bounded measurable \(f\), however wild. This is the same as squeezing \(f\) between two simple functions that are constant on each preimage.

The key difference is what each method needs. Riemann needs the values of \(f\) on intervals. Lebesgue needs the measure of the sets \(\{f>y\}\), which can be complicated sets, not intervals. Our numerical Lebesgue computes those measures exactly: on each monotone piece of \(f\) it finds the point where \(f\) crosses the level by bisection, and adds up the lengths. It does not estimate them by sampling.

Ex.1 · Where the two approaches disagree

The Darboux gap \(U_N-L_N\) as \(N\) grows, computed exactly (no sampling) for the four interesting cases. If the gap goes to 0 the function is Riemann-integrable; if it stays positive, only Lebesgue can integrate it.

Riemann ✗ · Lebesgue = 0

Dirichlet \(1_{\mathbb Q}\)

Every interval contains rationals and irrationals, so \(\sup=1\), \(\inf=0\) on every column: \(L_N=0\), \(U_N=1\) for every \(N\). For Lebesgue, \(\{f>y\}=\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]\) for \(0\le y<1\): a countable set, measure 0. The integral is 0.

A computer cannot help here: every float is rational, so the midpoint sum returns 1. The value 0 comes from the measure, which is a theorem (countable sets are null), not from any sample.

Riemann ✓ · Lebesgue = 0

Thomae (popcorn)

\(f(p/q)=1/q\), 0 on irrationals. \(\inf=0\) everywhere, and we compute \(\sup\) on each column exactly: it is \(1/q_{\min}\), where \(q_{\min}\) is the smallest denominator of a fraction inside the column. \(U_N\to0\), slowly, roughly like \(\log N/N\): integrable, with integral 0, even though \(f\) is discontinuous at every rational.

Riemann ✗ · Lebesgue = ½

Fat Cantor set \(1_C\)

Smith–Volterra–Cantor set: from \([0,1]\) remove, at step \(k\), an open middle piece of length \(4^{-k}\) from each of the \(2^{k-1}\) remaining intervals. What is left contains no interval, yet has measure \(1-\sum_k 2^{k-1}4^{-k}=\tfrac12\). So \(L_N=0\) always, \(U_N\to\tfrac12\): the gap never closes. Lebesgue: the integral is \(\mu(C)=\tfrac12\).

Unlike Dirichlet, here the "bad" set has positive measure, which is why Riemann fails: Riemann integrability means the discontinuities form a null set (Lebesgue's criterion).

improper Riemann · ordinary Lebesgue

\(1/\sqrt{x}\) on \((0,1]\)

Unbounded: the first column has \(\sup=\infty\), so \(U_N=\infty\) and the Riemann integral exists only as an improper limit. For Lebesgue, \(\mu(\{f>y\})=\min(1,1/y^2)\) is integrable over \([0,\infty)\) and gives 2 directly. Numerically we stop the layers at a height \(T\), and the missing tail is exactly \(1/T\).

Ex.2 · Why Φ has no formula

$$\Phi(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\varphi(t)\,dt,\qquad \varphi(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-t^2/2}.$$

Liouville's theorem (1835)

Elementary functions are those built from polynomials, \(\exp\), \(\log\), roots and trigonometric functions with finitely many operations and compositions. Liouville proved a criterion, sharpened by Rosenlicht (1972): if \(r,s\) are rational functions and \(s\) is not constant, then

$$\int r(t)\,e^{s(t)}\,dt \text{ is elementary} \iff \exists\,R \text{ rational with } R'+s'R=r .$$

Here \(r=1\), \(s=-t^2/2\), so we would need a rational \(R\) with \(R'-tR=1\).

  1. No poles. If \(R\) had a pole of order \(k\) at \(c\), then \(R'\) has one of order \(k+1\) and \(tR\) at most \(k\): the left side would have a pole, the right side (1) has none. So \(R\) is a polynomial.
  2. Wrong degree. If \(R\) is a polynomial of degree \(d\ge0\), \(tR\) has degree \(d+1\) and \(R'\) less, so \(R'-tR\) has degree \(d+1\ge1\), never the constant 1. And \(R=0\) gives 0, not 1.

No such \(R\) exists, so \(\int e^{-t^2/2}dt\) is not elementary, and neither is Φ. The Risch algorithm turns this kind of argument into a decision procedure used by computer algebra systems.

erf: a name, not an algorithm

The error function gives Φ an exact closed form:

$$\operatorname{erf}(z)=\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^z e^{-u^2}du,\qquad \Phi(x)=\tfrac12\big[1+\operatorname{erf}(x/\sqrt2)\big].$$

This is a real gain: erf has known derivatives, series, asymptotics and identities, so Φ can be manipulated symbolically. But naming an integral does not compute it. To get digits, someone still has to evaluate erf with an algorithm: quadrature, series, continued fractions or rational fits.

This is not special to erf. Even "elementary" \(e^x\) or \(\sin x\) have no finite formula in the four operations: your CPU's math library evaluates them with range reduction and polynomial approximations. The difference between "elementary" and "special" is about symbolic closure, not about numerical difficulty.

Ex.2 · Four ways to compute Φ

All methods use the symmetry \(\Phi(-x)=1-\Phi(x)\) and \(\Phi(0)=\tfrac12\), so they only need the integral from 0 to \(|x|\) or the right tail \(Q(x)=1-\Phi(x)\). Note that the left tail \(\Phi(-x)\) for large \(x\) is a tiny number: computing it as \(\tfrac12-(\text{something close to }\tfrac12)\) destroys its relative accuracy.

A · Adaptive Simpson (numerical integration)

Simpson on \([0,|x|]\), but each panel is split in two only where the two estimates disagree more than a tolerance \(\varepsilon\), with Richardson correction \(\tfrac{1}{15}(S_2-S_1)\). Effort goes where \(\varphi\) curves most. Then \(\Phi(x)=\tfrac12\pm\int_0^{|x|}\varphi\).

B · Gauss–Legendre quadrature (numerical integration)

\(\int_0^{|x|}\varphi\approx\sum_k w_k\varphi(t_k)\) with the nodes at the zeros of the Legendre polynomial \(P_n\). An \(n\)-point rule is exact for polynomials of degree \(2n-1\), so for a smooth integrand the error drops faster than any power of \(n\). Nodes and weights are computed at load time by Newton's method on \(P_n\), and the interval is split into panels of width ≤ 1.

C · Marsaglia's positive series

Integrating by parts repeatedly gives (Marsaglia, 2004)

$$\Phi(x)=\tfrac12+\varphi(x)\left(x+\frac{x^3}{3}+\frac{x^5}{3\cdot5}+\frac{x^7}{3\cdot5\cdot7}+\cdots\right),$$

where each term is the previous one times \(x^2/(2k+1)\). Unlike the Taylor series of erf, whose terms alternate in sign and cancel catastrophically for \(|x|\gtrsim3\), all these terms are positive: no cancellation.

D · Laplace's continued fraction (tails)

$$Q(x)=1-\Phi(x)=\cfrac{\varphi(x)}{x+\cfrac{1}{x+\cfrac{2}{x+\cfrac{3}{x+\cdots}}}},\qquad x>0 .$$

It converges fast exactly where the other methods struggle, for large \(x\), and returns the tail itself, so \(\Phi(-x)=Q(x)\) keeps full relative accuracy even at \(10^{-23}\). Evaluated bottom-up with \(K\) levels.

Hybrid · our final method

Series C for \(|x|<2\), continued fraction D for \(|x|\ge2\) with \(K=\lceil 40+320/x^2\rceil\) levels, each used where it is accurate, with the tail computed directly instead of as \(1-\Phi\).

Bonus · From Φ to normal random numbers

In the lecture of 5 November: a uniform \(U\) on \([0,1]\) becomes a sample of any distribution through its inverse CDF, \(X=\Phi^{-1}(U)\). With an accurate Φ we can invert it by Newton's method, \(x\leftarrow x-\frac{\Phi(x)-u}{\varphi(x)}\), using our hybrid Φ. Where Φ is steep (around 0), many values of \(u\) land in a short x-interval: that is where the samples pile up.

09

Conclusions

Riemann vs Lebesgue

On every Riemann-integrable function of the list both methods converge to the same value: the Darboux brackets and the layer brackets close on the same number (section 03). They differ in how they get there. The midpoint Riemann sum is the cheapest and most accurate on smooth functions (error ∝ 1/N²). Our Lebesgue computes exact preimages, so it is very accurate per level, but each level costs a root search: in evaluations it is the more expensive route.

They part ways on functions whose bad points are dense: Dirichlet and the fat Cantor indicator keep a Darboux gap of 1 and ½ forever, while Lebesgue gives 0 and ½. Thomae shows the boundary of Riemann's theory: discontinuous on a dense set, but a null one, so still integrable. And the Lebesgue answers for the pathological cases come from measure theory, not from sampling, because a computer only ever sees rational numbers.

The normal CDF

Liouville's theorem forbids an elementary formula for Φ. erf gives it an exact name, but numbers still require an algorithm. Both quadratures reach machine precision in the centre, but they give Φ(−x) as ½ minus an integral, which loses all relative accuracy in the left tail. The positive series avoids the cancellation of the erf series, and the continued fraction handles the tails directly. The hybrid keeps a relative error of about \(10^{-15}\) from −10 to 10 at a fraction of a microsecond per call (section 07).

Implementation notes

  • Exact Darboux sums. Each function lists its monotone pieces and jumps, so inf and sup on a column are taken at the column ends, at interior breakpoints and at one-sided limits. For Thomae the sup is \(1/q_{\min}\), found by scanning denominators.
  • Exact preimages. \(\mu(\{f>y\})\) is the total length of the parts of each monotone piece above \(y\), found by bisection to machine precision (counted as evaluations). For Dirichlet, Thomae and the fat Cantor set, marked measure oracle, the measure comes from the theorem (countable sets are null, \(\mu(C)=\tfrac12\)), since it cannot be computed from point values.
  • Fat Cantor set. Membership and "does a column meet C" are decided by descending the construction to depth 26 (removed pieces shorter than \(10^{-15}\)).
  • Gauss–Legendre. Nodes and weights computed at start-up by Newton's method on the three-term recurrence of \(P_n\).
  • Reference. mpmath.ncdf with 50 digits, embedded as a table; timings with performance.now() over repeated calls.
  • No chart library. All plots are drawn by hand on Canvas 2D; only KaTeX is loaded for formulas.
